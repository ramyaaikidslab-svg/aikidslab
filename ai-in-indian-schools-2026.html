AI in Indian schools, 2026: what changed this year and what it asks of your school

In twelve months, AI went from an optional extra to something a CBSE school is expected to teach from Class 3. Here is what changed nationally and in Telangana, what school leaders are discussing in Hyderabad this month, and the practical question underneath all of it: how do you deliver it well?

If 2025 was the year everyone talked about AI in schools, 2026 is the year it arrived in the timetable. Below is a short, sourced round-up for principals and academic coordinators — national policy first, then Telangana and Hyderabad — followed by what it all means in practice.

1. CBSE's AI curriculum now starts in Class 3

On 1 April 2026, Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan launched a curriculum on computational thinking and artificial intelligence for Classes 3 to 8, prepared with NCERT and applicable from the 2026–27 session (Careers360, IANS via Morung Express). For Classes 3 to 5, computational thinking is woven into mathematics, languages and environmental studies through puzzles, games and storytelling; Classes 6 to 8 add foundational AI concepts. Three documents came with it: the curriculum, a teachers' handbook and teaching-learning material, and CBSE later released resource books for each class.

Two details matter for schools. First, CBSE set "Computational Thinking and Understanding AI" as the teacher-training theme for the current session (AACRAO Edge). Second, look at the method the board chose for the youngest children: games, puzzles and stories. The board is saying, in effect, that AI should be learnt by doing, not memorised.

For Class 9, Artificial Intelligence (subject code 417) continues as a skill subject, which means the students who started computational thinking in Class 3 will eventually arrive in Class 9 expecting far more than a chapter.

2. The India AI Impact Summit put education on the main stage

In February 2026, India hosted the AI Impact Summit at Bharat Mandapam in New Delhi (overview). The Ministry of Education ran its own session there, framed around two themes: "AI in Education" and "Education in AI" (PIB release). Teaching children about AI and teaching with AI are now being treated as one agenda, and students took part in large numbers. Teachers from Hyderabad were there too: The Indian School reported its principals and teachers attending a pre-summit event whose education session argued for a human-centric approach to AI in classrooms (The Indian School).

3. 50,000 more Atal Tinkering Labs

The government's plan to set up 50,000 new Atal Tinkering Labs in government schools over five years now has real money behind it: the 2026–27 budget proposes ₹3,200 crore for the scheme (Careers360). As of October 2025, around 10,000 labs were already running. More labs mean more schools with the space to build things; the open question for every lab is what students actually build in it.

4. Telangana: AI literacy in government schools

Closer to home, Telangana has been one of the most active states. At the Global AI Summit in Hyderabad in September 2024, the state released a strategy that included an AI curriculum for senior secondary students (Deccan Herald). In 2026 the state, with Amazon Future Engineer and the Pi Jam Foundation, is bringing structured AI learning to government school students — reported as around a million students in Classes 6 to 9, about 25 hours a year, on an SCERT-designed curriculum, with more than 28,000 teachers trained (YourStory).

When government schools in the same city offer structured AI learning, "we cover AI" stops being a differentiator for a private school. What parents will compare is depth: what their child can actually do.

5. What Hyderabad's school leaders are discussing this month

AI is on the agenda of the city's school-leader events this October. The South India Educators' Summit in Hyderabad listed AI in education among its themes for school owners, principals and academic heads (EducationToday), and a school leaders' meetup at Sreenidhi International School on 9 October is built around leadership in the AI era (Toddle). The questions we hear from principals in these rooms are consistent: Who will teach it? How do we train teachers? How do we keep children safe? And how do we show parents it's real?

What it all adds up to

Read together, these developments point the same way:

  • From coverage to competence. A syllabus line is no longer enough. Boards, states and parents are asking what children can do.
  • Learning by doing. CBSE chose games, puzzles and stories for the youngest learners. Older students deserve at least as much play and building.
  • Teachers at the centre. Training is a theme everywhere, from CBSE's training theme to Telangana's teacher programme.
  • Responsible AI. Ethics, safety and human oversight run through all of it.

Where AI Kids Lab fits

We built our program for exactly this moment, and it lines up with each of these directions. It is gamified and hands-on, in the spirit of the puzzles-and-games approach CBSE chose. It follows the CBSE AI curriculum for Grades 7, 8 and 9, including AI (417) in Class 9. Teachers are trained through hands-on workshops. An engineer from a real product team is on campus every month, and the annual Build-a-thon gives Atal Tinkering Lab schools a natural showcase. Safety is built in: no student ever needs a personal AI account.

Schools that run the full program earn the AI-Enabled School badge — a verified mark, renewed every year, that tells parents and admissions visitors the school is genuinely doing this, not just saying it. (It is AI Kids Lab's own certification, not a government one.)

A five-question audit for 2026–27

Who teaches AI in each grade, and how were they trained? How many periods a year does each grade get? What does a student build, and how is it assessed? How do students use AI tools without personal accounts? What can you show a parent at the end of the year? If any answer is "we're not sure yet", that's the place to start.
