Young innovators

Every school has children like this. Most never find out.

Eight students who built something real before finishing school. A wearable for a grandmother with Alzheimer's. A thresher that stopped a classroom coughing. Heart screening that runs in seven seconds. Read what each of them was actually given — then count how much of it your school already has.

Eight cases

What a fourteen-year-old is actually capable of.

Every case below is sourced and linked. Read them not as miracles but as a specification: each child had a problem they had seen with their own eyes, an adult who took the work seriously, and somewhere to take it when it was finished. Three things. A school can supply all three, to four hundred students at a time.

Age 14

Siddharth Nandyala

Indian-origin · Frisco, Texas

Built Circadian AI, an app that listens to a heartbeat recorded on an ordinary smartphone and flags possible cardiovascular disease in about seven seconds. He built it in roughly eight months.

Reported accuracy above 96%, tested across roughly 15,000 patients in the US and 3,500 in India.
Source: Smithsonian Magazine
Started at 12

Hemesh Chadalavada

Jubilee Hills Public School · Hyderabad

His grandmother left the gas on after making tea. He built the Alpha Monitor, a wearable that tracks wandering, pulse and temperature for a person living with Alzheimer's and alerts a carer over long-range radio.

Won the Pradhan Mantri Rashtriya Bal Puraskar at 13, and the Samsung Solve for Tomorrow contest with a $40,000 grant.
Source: The Better India
From Class 8

Pooja Pal

Village school · Barabanki, Uttar Pradesh

She watched children cough in the dust thrown up by a wheat thresher near her school. With her science teacher's guidance and a local welder, she built a dust-free thresher for about ₹3,000.

Recognised through INSPIRE-MANAK and the Bal Puraskar; flew to Japan on the Sakura Science programme, the only selection from her state. A patent is in progress.
Source: Global Indian
Started at 13

Akash Manoj

Hosur, Tamil Nadu

After losing his grandfather to a silent heart attack, he spent three years in labs developing a non-invasive patch that detects h-FABP, a marker the body releases up to six hours before an attack.

Awarded at Intel ISEF 2018; the work drew clinical interest from AIIMS Delhi, the Royal Society of Medicine and Tokyo University of Science.
Source: Forbes India
Age 14

Vinisha Umashankar

Tiruvannamalai, Tamil Nadu

Saw a street ironing vendor dumping charcoal, researched why, and designed a solar-powered ironing cart — five hours of sun runs the iron for six, removing the smoke that damages vendors' lungs.

Finalist for the inaugural Earthshot Prize in 2021, its youngest, and spoke at COP26 in Glasgow.
Source: The Earthshot Prize
Age 14

Harshwardhan Zala

Ahmedabad, Gujarat

Watched a news report on soldiers killed defusing landmines by hand, and spent three years building EAGLE A7, a drone that finds metal and plastic landmines and detonates them remotely.

Signed a memorandum of understanding reported at ₹5 crore with the Government of Gujarat in 2017, and founded Aerobotics7.
Source: Forbes India
School student

Mohnish Kumar Dhruv

Bilaspur, Chhattisgarh

Built the Atal Divyang Rath, a chair-cum-vehicle that lets a person with a physical disability use a washroom without assistance — developed through his school's Atal Tinkering Lab.

Won the National INSPIRE Award and presented the project to the Prime Minister during National Technology Week 2023.
Source: Atal Tinkering Lab case studies
Invented it at 11

Gitanjali Rao

Indian-origin · Colorado, USA

Built Tethys, a device using carbon nanotubes to detect lead in drinking water and send readings over Bluetooth, prompted by the Flint water crisis.

Named TIME magazine's first-ever Kid of the Year in 2020, at 15, chosen from more than 5,000 nominees.
Source: USPTO Young Inventors

Details are as reported by the sources linked on each card; ages are at the time of the work described. AI Kids Lab has no affiliation with these students and claims no credit for their achievements.

The pattern

Read the eight again and the same four things appear.

  • A problem they had seen themselves. A grandmother leaving the gas on. Children coughing in thresher dust. A grandfather's death. Not one of them began with "I want to learn AI."
  • Years, not weeks. Akash Manoj spent three years. Harshwardhan Zala spent three. A workshop cannot produce this. A three-year curriculum can.
  • One adult who took it seriously. Pooja Pal's thresher exists because a science teacher in a village school did not dismiss it. That teacher is the whole story, and every school has one.
  • Somewhere to take it. A competition, a panel, a prize. Work with nowhere to go stops in June, whatever the child is capable of.
Ability at fourteen is not rare. Being noticed at fourteen is.
Why this matters commercially

What it does for the three numbers you are actually judged on.

A program that produces students like the ones above is not a feelgood exercise. It moves admissions, retention and referrals — and it does so through mechanisms you can name to a board, not vague goodwill.

Admissions

Something a competitor cannot copy by Tuesday

New enrolments

Any school can buy a robotics lab and put it in the brochure. No school can manufacture "one of our Grade 9s built a tool her grandmother actually uses, and presented it to a panel of engineers." Every article about Hemesh Chadalavada names his school. That is the sentence that ends a parent tour.

What to watch: how often the AI program comes up unprompted on a tour, and how many enquiries mention it by name.
Retention

A reason to still be here in Grade 9

Students who stay

Transfers cluster at grade transitions, and they are easiest when a child has nothing unfinished. A student halfway through a three-year arc has a capstone ahead of them, a mentor who knows their work, and a Build-a-thon they intend to win. Leaving costs them something real.

What to watch: Grade 8 to 9 retention against your own baseline, and whether leavers cite the program as a reason to stay.
NPS & referrals

Parents recommend what they have watched

Word of mouth

A report card does not travel between parents. A parent who watched their own child stand up and defend something they built, in front of strangers, tells other parents that week. The Build-a-thon exists partly for this reason: it is the highest-signal hour in your school year, and parents are in the room.

What to watch: your NPS in the fortnight after the Build-a-thon, and referrals attributed to parents who attended.
How a school supplies it

Turning a lottery into a pipeline.

Your school already has the students. What is usually missing is the sequence that carries a fourteen-year-old from a vague idea to something a stranger will take seriously.

01

Every student gets a shot at being noticed

The curriculum runs for the whole grade, not a club, so the quiet student in the third row produces a project too. In our experience that student is the surprise about as often as the obvious one.

02

The problem has to be theirs

Capstones start from something the student has actually seen — at home, on their street, in their grandparents' lives. That one rule separates a project that ends in June from one they keep working on.

03

A real practitioner, on your campus

The scarcest ingredient in all eight stories is an adult who builds for a living and takes a student's work seriously. Your school should not have to go find that person; we bring one every month.

04

Somewhere for the work to go

Your annual Build-a-thon, then national competitions, then — for the strongest Grade 9 builds — an introduction to the student incubation labs we collaborate with.
